Behaviour Button 1.0 / 2023

Designing the whole
participant experience.

The button was one part of a wider experience: receiving the kit, making sense of its parts, setting it up and using it in everyday life.

  1. 01

    Around October 2023

    The starting point

    A study that could arrive by post.

    Behaviour Button began around October 2023. My role was to design, develop and deploy a pilot exploring a new time-use survey experience.

    The first version used Shortcut Labs’ Flic Hub LR and the then-alpha version of its Device Manager. We wanted participants to receive a complete kit by post and set up the devices themselves.

    That goal made the packaging and instructions a central part of the design work.

    The design question How could the kit help someone get started without us being there?

  2. 02

    Early packaging exploration

    Protection, presentation and instructions

    Making the first encounter playful.

    I explored ways to keep the hub secure during transport while making it fun and straightforward to take out of the box.

    One insert held the Hub LR at an angle so it could be lifted out more easily, with the buttons grouped along one side. Numbered compartments, opening flaps and setup illustrations explored how the packaging could guide the participant.

    The angled approach was enjoyable, but it required a lot of assembly by hand and did not hold the equipment securely enough.

    More packaging experiments +
  3. 03

    Exploring other approaches

    Fit, form and assembly

    Trying different ways to make the kit.

    I could laser-cut the angled inserts, but the amount of manual assembly made them difficult to scale. I explored a 3D-printed mould for vacuum forming, more pronounced three-dimensional shapes with a luxury presentation, and a modular approach.

    These were different ways of balancing how the kit looked and felt with how securely it held the parts and how it could be made.

    Fit studies and annotated prototypes +
  4. 04

    The first kit version

    Cardboard and EVA foam

    Choosing what worked for this version.

    After more trial and error, we arrived at a kit combining cardboard and EVA foam. We knew this approach would not scale, but chose it for the playful presentation and secure fit it offered at this stage.

    The insert brought the hub, cables, power adaptor and buttons together. A yellow printed surface labelled the parts and numbered the buttons, with a connection diagram inside the lid.

    The decision A fun, secure first kit, with the limits on production scale understood.

    Inside the first kit +
  5. 05

    Alongside the kit development

    Software tests in everyday settings

    Coffee, the fridge and a button press.

    At the same time, we placed a button at the office coffee maker and another on the fridge to test the software backend through everyday activities.

    Activity icons and short instructions explained when and how to press the buttons. We also explored laser engraving serial numbers onto the button bodies.

    The office setup and engraving exploration +
  6. 06

    November 2023

    The first participant study

    Taking the experience into a six-person pilot.

    Later that year, we ran a pilot with six participants. As an industrial designer, my main focus was the user experience of the kit, including its packaging, instructions and setup journey.

    Insight gathering and data analysis were limited in this first pilot.

    The participant instructions asked for a narrated video of unboxing and installation, and photographs showing the installed hub and buttons in their surroundings. These requests focused attention on how the kit fitted into someone’s home.

Behaviour Button 2.0 / Development from 2024

Giving feedback
a physical home.

Learning from the first kit and pilot shaped a hub with visible feedback. Seven major designs explored its form, structure and production.

  1. 07

    2024 / Behaviour Button 2.0

    Participant feedback

    Deciding what the hub should show.

    The first version and its pilot showed us that participants needed a way to keep track of their engagement. The buttons offered limited feedback, so I considered how the hub could help someone check that a press had registered.

    The interface showed a short timeline of recent presses and an overall press count. Detailed activity analysis was left until after the study. This was a deliberate choice intended to limit how the feedback might influence behaviour, including the risk of confirmation bias.

    The design intention Give reassurance during the study, with detailed reflection afterwards.

    During the study

    Check the system.

    • A short timeline of recent presses
    • An overall press count
    • Feedback for troubleshooting and review
    After the study

    Reflect on the detail.

    • Detailed activity analysis
    • Counts for individual activities
    • For example, how often the snack button was pressed
  2. 08

    Early 2.0 hub concepts

    Designs 1–3

    Exploring the display and the object.

    I began with three different relationships between the screen, enclosure and computing hardware: a frosted enclosure that would reveal its internals, a bulkier kitchen-appliance direction, and a thin display above a heavier computing base.

    The prototypes explored how the hub might look and feel in a home. Their visible screen content illustrates interface ideas.

    Designs 1–7 are the major hub concepts. Version numbers describe revisions within a design.

  3. 09

    Design 4

    Establishing the structure

    The direction begins to settle.

    Design 4 brought together a 4.26-inch e-ink display, a Raspberry Pi 4 behind it and a kickstand to present the screen at an angle. I drew inspiration from the iMac G4 for the clear acrylic front, but laminating the display to the acrylic with resin was difficult to achieve and repeat.

    The internal sandwich structure carried into later designs. A bottom base layer and an Integrated Truss Structure supported the Pi and display and provided routes for the USB-C and RJ45 cables. The ISS-inspired part names carried forward too.

  4. 10

    Design 5

    Connections and enclosure layout

    Bringing the cables into the design.

    I remodelled the exterior using what I had learned from Design 4. A frosted acrylic label at the upper rear held the hardware information and serial number.

    Participants in the first pilot had wanted built-in cables to avoid uncertainty about which ones to connect. I incorporated cables exiting through the rear stand. I also recorded the assembled hub while calibrating the visible display area around the case.

  5. 11

    Design 6

    Printability and cost

    Refining the build.

    Design 6 stayed close to Design 5, and I no longer remember why I gave it a new major number. I was fairly happy with the direction and focused on printability and the bill of materials.

    We removed the acrylic rear strip because of cost, even though it was one of my favourite features. I made a few hubs for the developers to work on the firmware and continued exploring the packaging.

  6. 12

    The slim branch / Design 7

    Reworking the internals

    Making the slim form work.

    Around revision 6.3, the frame became noticeably slimmer. This led to an asymmetric branch within Design 6 that I called Design 6.4 Slim. People liked the form, which encouraged me to rework the internal arrangement.

    A pause while a development backlog was addressed gave me time for that work. It led to Design 7, the final symmetrical thin-frame design, through repeated small adjustments to the internal layout. It took a lot of coffee, and I felt the result was worth the effort.

  7. 13

    The wider 2.0 project

    The team and the public pilot

    Designing with a wider team.

    Alongside the hub development, NICA’s Dev and Commercial teams supported the backend, participant management and pilot design. With that wider support, we launched a public pilot with 20 participants. My focus was designing and making the kits, particularly the hub.

    We ultimately made 25 hubs for the project.

  8. 14

    Future directions

    Ambitions for the platform

    A foundation for further research tools.

    I wanted the Raspberry Pi hub to offer a flexible foundation for different sensors and communication methods. Our future directions included additional connections such as ZigBee, Wi-Fi and LoRaWAN, and relaying data from wearable research devices.

    We also saw possibilities for offline, local machine learning and Edge AI, including recognition, tagging and counting. These remained ambitions for future development.
